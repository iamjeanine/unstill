## THE GLASS PLATES — WHAT THEY ARE

The Special Photographs are glass plate negatives. Approximately 2,500 were taken between 1910 and the early 1930s. They are not mugshots — mugshots are taken at the prison after conviction. The Specials were taken at Central Police Station immediately after arrest, before trial, before conviction. The people in them have not yet been found guilty of anything.

### The yard
Most of the Specials were taken in the yards at the rear of Central Police Station, next to the Central court complex in the central business district of Sydney. The dark surface visible behind the subjects in the photographs is the yard wall. A wooden bench where prisoners waited is visible in some frames. The space was an exercise yard — open air but enclosed by high walls.

Inspector William MacKay toured European and American police stations in 1928 and wrote a report in 1929 calling Central Police Station "totally unsuited to meet modern requirements" and "obsolete." He noted: there was no room to interview a complainant or prisoner privately. Suspects were detained on a four-foot bench in the charge room in full view of the public. The identification line-up took place in the exercise yard at the rear of the cells. Witnesses called to identify suspects had to walk through cell corridors in view of large numbers of prisoners, and were "frequently unnerved in the process."

### The bentwood chair
A bentwood chair appears in most photographs alongside the subject. It was placed in the frame to give a sense of the person's height. It is a measurement tool, not furniture. It is not for sitting.

### How the subjects were photographed
Unlike prison mugshots, the subjects of the Specials were "allowed — perhaps invited — to position and compose themselves for the camera as they liked." Curator Peter Doyle: "Their photographic identity thus seems constructed out of a potent alchemy of inborn disposition, personal history, learned habits and idiosyncrasies, chosen personal style (haircut, clothing, accessories) and physical characteristics." They were "men and women recently plucked from the street, often still animated by the dramas surrounding their apprehension."

They are wearing what they were wearing when they were arrested.

### The photographer
The photographer was likely George Howard, noted in contemporary newspapers for "artistic proclivities." He gave his subjects a kind of agency the justice system had taken away — the right to present themselves.

### The inscriptions
The photographer was responsible for recording vital information with each image. This evolved into a system where the date, the suspect's name and fingerprint classification, and sometimes a reference to a previous police file were inscribed onto the emulsion (reverse) side of the glass plate negative. Police mastered the technique of writing back to front so that the information was legible in the photographic print.

### Cropping and editing
"Photobombing" police or other detainees appearing in the background were literally whited out of the printed image, which was reduced to a tight crop. Today, digital technology allows examination of details in the negatives that were removed from or indistinct in surviving prints — the texture of a coat, a teardrop, a medal on a returned serviceman's chest.

### What they were for
The photographs were never intended for public display or consumption. They were internal police tools, meant to familiarise officers with the criminal milieu — to help police recognise these people on the street. No handcuffs or signs of custody are visible, so the images could be shown to witnesses during investigations without prejudicing them against the suspect.

### Storage, damage, and rediscovery
The glass plates were stored in wooden chests in police storage for decades, forgotten. In 1990, after a storm, New South Wales police checked on the storage space. Water had seeped into the wooden chests and an ominous sludge had formed at the base of each. The effects of water, clumsy handling, and natural ageing mark the negatives — losses, chips, silvering, and fingerprints have become part of the archive's story.

130,000 negatives survive in the NSW Police Forensic Photography Archive. 52,000 searchable records are now online. Of the 2,500 Specials, only a handful have been exhibited or published. The vast majority have never been seen.

Peter Doyle curated the "City of Shadows" exhibition at the Justice & Police Museum in 2005 — the most popular exhibition ever held at the museum. Nerida Campbell published "Femme Fatale: The Female Criminal" (2008) and "Underworld: Mugshots from the Roaring Twenties."

### What the glass plate knows
The glass plate knows: where it was made (the yard), who made it (probably Howard), what it was for (police identification), how the person was recorded (name scratched backwards on the emulsion), how it was stored (wooden chest), what damaged it (water, handling, time), and that it was never meant to be seen by anyone other than police. It does not know who the person is. It knows the conditions under which the person was photographed.

## THE LAWS THAT MADE THEM CRIMINAL

Before 1927, cocaine was sold legally at pharmacies across Sydney. The Dangerous Drugs Amendment Act 1927 made its sale illegal overnight. The same substance, the same people, the same behaviour — suddenly criminal. An estimated 5,000 cocaine users lived in Sydney at the time. On one day they were customers. The next they were offenders.

The Pistol Licensing Act 1927 imposed severe penalties for carrying concealed firearms. Sydney's criminals switched to straight razors — cheap from any barber shop, easy to conceal inside a piece of cork, capable of disfiguring a face in one stroke. Over 500 slashings were recorded in three years. The press renamed Darlinghurst: Razorhurst.

Six o'clock closing (the Licensing Act 1916) shut every pub at 6pm. The sly-grog industry — illegal drinking establishments — exploded. Kate Leigh ran over 20 sly-grog shops at her peak, catering to all classes.

The Vagrancy Act 1905 made street sex work illegal. But a loophole in the Police Offences Amendment Act 1908 made it illegal only for men to live off sex work earnings. The lawmakers assumed no woman would run brothels. Tilly Devine saw this as an invitation.

The Vagrancy Amendment Act 1929 made it illegal to "habitually consort with reputed thieves, prostitutes, or persons who have no visible means of support." You could be arrested for standing next to the wrong person. Knowing someone was the crime. Nellie Cameron became the first woman in Australia convicted under this law.

Every single law designed to protect public morality created an industry. Prohibition of cocaine created drug trafficking. Prohibition of late drinking created sly grog. Prohibition of street sex work created organised brothels. Prohibition of firearms created razor gangs. The people in these photographs were standing at the intersection of all of them.

## DARLINGHURST — THE SIX BLOCKS

The entire underworld operated within a few blocks. Kings Cross, Darlinghurst, Surry Hills, Woolloomooloo, and Paddington were sprawling slums of unsanitary, ramshackle Victorian terraces and makeshift shacks. The Truth newspaper described it: "Razorhurst, Gunhurst, Bottlehurst, Dopehurst — it used to be Darlinghurst, one of the finest quarters of a rich and beautiful city; today it is a plague spot where the spawn of the gutter grow and fatten on official apathy."

Palmer Street was the centre of Tilly Devine's brothel empire — at her height she ran 18 establishments in the area. William Street (where Marie Elliott ran her cocaine flat) was three blocks from the courthouse, two from the theatre district. Everyone knew everyone. Your dealer, your arresting officer, and the magistrate who sentenced you could all live on the same street.

Cocaine runners followed the sex workers through the streets. SP bookmakers worked the back rooms of sly-grog shops. The entertainment overlapped with the criminal — theatre crowds bought cocaine from the same people who sold to addicts. The boundaries between respectable Sydney and the underworld were porous. Professionals slummed in Darlinghurst; criminals dressed for the races.

Today these same streets contain million-dollar apartments, galleries, and designer cafes. The most dangerous thing that could happen to you is stepping in dog mess. Nothing visible remains of Razorhurst.

## THE COCAINE TRADE

Before 1927, cocaine was sold openly by pharmacists on prescription. Most of the illicit supply was smuggled from Asia by Chinese and local sailors, who sold in bulk to distributors. Kate Leigh — "the Snow Queen" — was the city's biggest cocaine dealer. Phil "the Jew" Jeffs was another major distributor who ran his operation from sly-grog shops disguised as social clubs (the 50-50 Club, the 400 Club).

The street price of cocaine rose dramatically after prohibition. The drug was sold as "snow" in small paper packets called "decks." Cocaine runners — often young women — carried it through the streets, delivering to sex workers and their clients, to partygoers, to workers. A runner might deliver to a dozen addresses in a night.

Patsy Neill was described as a barmaid — her night job was running cocaine for Marie Elliott. Henry Pierce watched the door while the operation ran from Marie's flat on William Street.

The police spent three years making arrests and seizures. Almost all of those arrested were "lowly street pedlars or relatively minor independent dealers." The major distributors — insulated from direct street contact — were immune. The people photographed were the visible layer of an invisible economy.

Phil Jeffs adulterated a cocaine shipment with boracic acid in 1929, sparking the Battle of Blood Alley — a half-hour brawl in Eaton Avenue involving twenty men with pistols, razors, and knuckle dusters. Jeffs was shot four times but survived.

By 1938-39, intensive policing of overseas supply routes finally began to reduce the trade. The Second World War finished it — gang figures enlisted, supply routes dried up, and a new era began.

## THE TWO QUEENS — TILLY DEVINE AND KATE LEIGH

Tilly Devine, "Queen of Woolloomooloo," was born in London in 1900, worked as a sex worker from her teenage years, married an Australian soldier, followed him to Sydney in 1920. She served two years for slashing a man with a razor, then decided to manage rather than work. She exploited the legal loophole allowing women to run brothels and built an empire of 18 establishments. Her husband Big Jim sold cocaine from the brothels.

Kate Leigh, "Queen of Surry Hills," born 1887 in Dubbo, was a sly-grogger, cocaine dealer (hence "the Snow Queen"), and fence for stolen goods. She ran over 20 illegal drinking establishments. For amusement, she shoplifted at Mark Foys department store.

The two women waged open warfare from 1929. Their gangs fought with razors, pistols, and knuckle dusters across the inner-city suburbs. They employed bodyguards, standover men, bouncers, runners, and informants.

In 1935, Police Commissioner MacKay summoned both to his office and offered a deal: stop the violence, and police would tolerate their businesses. They agreed. The razor wars ended not with law enforcement but with a negotiated truce between two crime bosses and the police.

Patsy Neill's disagreement with Kate Leigh over money — which ended with Leigh putting a gun in Neill's face — was a minor incident in this wider war. Nellie Cameron moved between both factions, lovers on each side, surviving longer than most.

## THE RAZOR — WEAPON OF CHOICE

After the Pistol Licensing Act made carrying a gun an automatic prison sentence, razors became the default weapon. A cut-throat razor cost a few pence from any barber shop. Concealed inside a piece of cork, it was invisible until used. It could disfigure a face permanently — a visible warning to anyone who saw the victim afterward.

The razor was an instrument of intimidation as much as violence. A slash across the cheek said: this happened to someone who crossed us. The scars were advertising. Frank Green — Nellie Cameron's lover — had an L-shaped razor scar on his right cheek. His body was riddled with bullet wounds when he died.

Guido Calletti, Nellie's husband, led the Darlinghurst Push — a small group of street brawlers and standover men. In 1933, Green and Calletti fought a bare-knuckle fist fight watched by 600 people to settle who got to be Nellie's lover. No clear winner. She stayed with Calletti.

By 1930, carrying a razor itself became illegal. Within months, the public violence dropped. The private violence continued behind closed doors.

## THE PHOTOGRAPHS — WHY THEY EXIST

See "THE GLASS PLATES — WHAT THEY ARE" at the top of this document for full detail on the physical photographs, the yard, the photographer, the inscriptions, storage, damage, and rediscovery. This section adds context about the photographs' place in the world.

The Specials are unlike any police photography found elsewhere in the world. Standard mugshots are front and profile, expressionless, clinical. The Specials gave their subjects something the system otherwise denied them: the right to present themselves.

The people in the photographs were suspected of a crime. Some had been charged. None had been convicted. They were photographed in the gap between arrest and trial — a legal limbo where they were neither free nor guilty.

The police published photo supplements in the NSW Police Gazette showing faces of wanted or recently arrested persons. The Specials were tools of surveillance that happened to capture something human.

## WOMEN IN THE SYSTEM

Women occupied every level of Sydney's underworld — from street-level sex workers and cocaine runners to the two most powerful crime figures in the city. But the law treated them differently.

Women earned roughly half what men earned. The basic male wage in the late 1920s was approximately £4 per week. Women in factory work or service jobs earned £2 or less. A barmaid like Patsy Neill might earn £2-3 per week legitimately. A cocaine runner could earn multiples of that in a night.

The consorting laws targeted women disproportionately. Being seen with known criminals was enough for arrest. For women in Darlinghurst, where everyone knew everyone, avoiding known criminals was nearly impossible — they were your neighbours, your customers, your partners.

The State Reformatory for Women at Long Bay was where several of these subjects served time. Nellie Cameron, Patsy Neill, and others were photographed there. Conditions were harsh — discipline-focused, with forced labour. Women convicted of drug offences or sex work were treated as moral failures, not economic actors.

The press treated criminal women as either fallen angels or monsters. Patsy Neill was described as "looking like a mannequin on parade." Nellie Cameron was "Sydney's Underworld Beauty Queen." Their appearance was always the story — not their choices, not their circumstances, not the economic logic of their decisions.

Edna May Lindsay's story cuts differently. She wasn't part of the underworld — she was 19, committed one crime (forgery), and was released with a warning. She dressed in polka dots and a cloche hat. The judge told her "dancing isn't everything." She appeared and disappeared from the record in a single day.

## MONEY — WHAT THINGS WERE WORTH

The basic male wage in late 1920s Australia was approximately £4 per week (roughly £200 per year). Women earned approximately half — about £2 per week.

A barmaid might earn £2-3 per week. A factory girl less. A domestic servant, often with room and board, earned even less in cash.

The forged cheque Edna Lindsay and her boyfriend attempted was reportedly for about £50 — approximately a year's wages for a working-class woman. They walked into a bank dressed like the money was already theirs.

Patsy Neill reportedly paid a fine of £250 — over a year's wages. Where that money came from tells you everything the charge sheet doesn't.

Cocaine, once legal and cheap at pharmacies, became expensive on the black market after 1927. A "deck" (small paper packet) sold for several shillings — a significant amount when a meal cost pennies. The profit margins were enormous, which is why people killed for control of the trade.

Tilly Devine's brothel empire generated enormous wealth. At her peak she owned multiple properties across East Sydney. The Australian Tax Department eventually ordered her to pay £20,000 in unpaid taxes — a fortune. She was forced to sell most of her properties. Kate Leigh suffered a similar decline.

## THE WORLD JUST BEFORE

The 1920s in Sydney were a period of rapid modernisation. Electric trams, motor cars, department stores, picture palaces, dance halls. The harbour bridge was under construction (completed 1932). Radio had arrived. Fashion was changing — women wore shorter skirts, bobbed their hair, danced the Charleston.

But the slums of the inner east had barely changed since the Victorian era. The contrast between modern Sydney and Razorhurst was extreme — polished city streets giving way to narrow lanes where bodies were dumped.

The Depression was approaching. By 1930-31, unemployment would reach 30%. The casual economy these people operated in — running cocaine, bartending, sex work, theft — was already the economy of people who had no access to the legitimate one.

Many of the men in these photographs had served in the First World War or were sons of men who did. The AIF connection runs through the families. Nellie's father served. The war had produced men comfortable with violence and uncomfortable with peace. Some became criminals. Some became police. Some became both.

## WHAT HAPPENED AFTER

The razor gang era ended through a combination of new laws, the Depression, and the Second World War. Gang figures enlisted or were imprisoned. The cocaine supply routes dried up. Tilly and Kate reached their negotiated truce.

By the 1950s, the inner east was changing. The slums were being cleared. New housing commission developments replaced the terraces. The criminal economy shifted to new substances, new locations, new figures.

Nellie Cameron died in 1953, aged 41, by her own hand. Kate Leigh died in 1964. Tilly Devine died in 1970, in poverty — her empire gone, her properties sold for tax debts. Frank Green was stabbed to death by his girlfriend in 1956.

Of the seven people in UNSTILL: we know what happened to Nellie (death by suicide, 1953) and to Henry, Patsy, and Marie only through their arrest records. Of Edna, Fay, and Elsie, nothing survives beyond the day they were photographed.

The photographs outlasted all of them. Glass plates in police storage, forgotten for decades, until someone thought to look.
